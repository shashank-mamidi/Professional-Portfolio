From Signal Noise to Model Error: Understanding AI/ML Uncertainty

A technical artifact exploring how noisy signals, ambiguous data, sensor limitations, analog front-end errors, data-conversion issues, model behavior, and hardware constraints can affect AI/ML decisions.

Back to Artifacts

Objective

Purpose of the Artifact

The purpose of this artifact is to examine why AI/ML systems sometimes produce uncertain, incorrect, or inconsistent decisions. The term “confusion” is treated here as a technical problem involving ambiguous inputs, noisy data, classification errors, model limitations, and uncertainty in the final output.

This artifact follows uncertainty through the complete signal path, from a physical event and sensor output to analog conditioning, data conversion, digital processing, and AI/ML inference.

It also connects AI/ML reliability to mixed-signal design by examining how noise, offset, distortion, limited dynamic range, quantization, sampling, and power-supply quality can influence the information presented to a model.

Overview

What Does “Confusion” Mean in AI/ML?

An AI/ML system becomes confused when the available information does not clearly support one decision. This can happen because two classes share similar features, the input is noisy, the training data is incomplete, or the system encounters a condition that was not represented during training.

Confusion can occur at different levels. The data may be unclear, the model may be unable to separate two classes, or the hardware may distort the signal before it reaches the model. These effects can combine and make it difficult to identify the original physical event.

A reliable AI/ML system therefore requires more than a high-performing model. It also requires a reliable sensor, a carefully designed signal chain, accurate data conversion, appropriate validation, and hardware that operates consistently under real-world conditions.

Sources of Uncertainty

Where Confusion Can Enter the System

Confusion can enter an AI/ML system at almost any stage of the signal and decision path. A model may appear to make a classification error, but the original cause may be a noisy sensor, an inaccurate analog front end, insufficient data conversion, incomplete training data, or limitations in the model itself.

Improving the model alone may not solve the problem. The complete system must be examined from the physical input through the final classification decision.

01

Ambiguous Physical Events

Different physical conditions may produce similar sensor outputs. For example, two machine faults may generate similar vibration patterns, making classification difficult.

When the characteristics of two classes overlap, even a well-designed model may have difficulty separating them reliably.

02

Sensor Noise and Environmental Variation

Sensors are affected by thermal noise, interference, drift, vibration, electromagnetic coupling, supply variation, and changes in the surrounding environment.

These effects can hide important signal features or create patterns that the model incorrectly interprets as meaningful information.

03

Sensor Calibration and Mismatch

Sensors of the same type may produce different outputs because of process variation, gain mismatch, offset, sensitivity differences, aging, or temperature dependence.

If calibration is incomplete, the model may receive different representations of the same physical event and produce inconsistent predictions.

04

Analog Front-End Limitations

The analog front end can introduce offset, gain error, distortion, insufficient bandwidth, clipping, or unwanted interference before the signal reaches the converter.

Poor filtering may allow unwanted frequencies into the signal, while excessive filtering may remove features important for classification.

05

Sampling and Aliasing

If a signal is sampled too slowly, higher-frequency content can appear as a lower-frequency component. This aliasing can create a misleading digital representation of the original physical signal.

Incorrect sampling timing or an unsuitable sampling rate can cause the model to learn features that do not accurately represent the real event.

06

Data Conversion Errors

ADC resolution, linearity, quantization noise, reference accuracy, and sampling behavior all influence the quality of converted data.

If the converter cannot represent small but important signal differences, two physically different conditions may appear identical to the AI/ML system.

07

Incomplete or Biased Training Data

A model can become confused when training data does not adequately represent the conditions it will encounter during deployment.

Missing operating conditions, unbalanced classes, incorrect labels, or limited environmental variation can cause the model to learn an incomplete view of the problem.

08

Feature Extraction Problems

Poorly chosen features may remove useful information, preserve irrelevant information, or make different classes appear more similar than they actually are.

09

Model Overfitting

An overfit model performs well on training data but fails to generalize to new examples. It may memorize specific patterns instead of learning the underlying behavior.

10

Out-of-Distribution Inputs

A model may receive an input significantly different from anything in its training data. It may still produce a confident prediction without enough information for a reliable decision.

11

Quantization and Hardware Approximation

Reduced-precision arithmetic can lower power, memory usage, and computational cost. However, quantization can change the values used during inference if not properly evaluated.

12

Power, Timing, and Thermal Effects

Supply variation, temperature, workload, clock frequency, timing errors, thermal drift, unstable power rails, and memory faults can affect AI/ML results during deployment.

System-Level Insight

Confusion Is Often a Chain Problem

Confusion in an AI/ML system is rarely caused by only one isolated block. Sensor noise may be amplified by an analog circuit, reduced by limited ADC resolution, treated as a feature during preprocessing, and then misinterpreted by the model.

System reliability therefore requires coordinated design across sensing, analog conditioning, data conversion, digital processing, model development, hardware implementation, and validation.

Evaluation

How Classification Performance Is Measured

Accuracy

Accuracy measures the overall percentage of correct predictions. It is most useful when the classes are reasonably balanced.

Precision

Precision measures how many positive predictions were actually correct. It is important when false alarms are costly.

Recall

Recall measures how many actual positive cases were detected. It is important when missing a real event has serious consequences.

Mixed-Signal Perspective

How Circuit-Level Design Influences AI Reliability

An AI model cannot recover information that has already been corrupted or lost before inference. If a sensor signal is distorted by noise, clipped by limited dynamic range, or poorly represented by the ADC, the model may receive an input that does not accurately represent the original physical event.

This makes the analog and mixed-signal front end an important part of AI system reliability. Circuit-level decisions can directly affect the quality of the features available to the model.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio

A higher signal-to-noise ratio makes it easier to distinguish meaningful features from unwanted variation.

Offset and Gain Error

Offset and gain errors can shift or scale the signal, potentially changing the features extracted by the AI/ML system.

Filtering and Bandwidth

Filtering can remove unwanted content, while adequate bandwidth ensures important signal information is not lost.

ADC Resolution

ADC resolution affects how finely the signal is represented and how much useful information is lost through quantization.

Reliability Improvements

Methods for Reducing AI/ML Uncertainty

Sensor Calibration

Calibration can reduce offset, gain variation, drift, and device-to-device differences before data is supplied to the model.

Analog Filtering

Appropriate filtering can reduce unwanted noise and prevent out-of-band signals from affecting sampled data.

Improved Data Quality

Representative, balanced, and well-labeled training data helps the model learn meaningful patterns rather than accidental correlations.

Confidence Thresholds

A system can flag low-confidence predictions instead of treating every model output as equally reliable.

Out-of-Distribution Detection

The system can identify inputs that differ significantly from training data and avoid making overconfident decisions.

Hardware-in-the-Loop Testing

Testing the model with real sensors and hardware can reveal errors that may not appear in software-only simulation.

Process

Process Used to Develop the Artifact

I developed this artifact by starting with the idea of “confusion” in AI/ML and translating it into technical concepts such as uncertainty, classification error, ambiguous data, and signal degradation.

I then traced possible sources of uncertainty through a complete sensor-to-inference signal chain. This included the sensor, analog front end, ADC, digital processing, model, and final classification output.

Finally, I connected these sources of uncertainty to mixed-signal design considerations such as noise, offset, bandwidth, dynamic range, quantization, power-supply quality, and sampling.

Value Proposition

Value of This Artifact

This artifact shows that AI/ML reliability depends on more than the model alone. It connects algorithmic performance to data quality, signal-chain design, hardware implementation, and deployment conditions.

It also demonstrates my ability to relate AI/ML concepts to analog and mixed-signal design. Noise, offset, filtering, data conversion, power integrity, and timing can all affect the quality of information presented to a model.

By examining uncertainty from the physical signal through the final decision, this artifact presents a system-level view of AI reliability and shows how circuit-level engineering can contribute to better AI/ML outcomes.

Summary

Key Takeaways

  • AI/ML confusion can result from ambiguous data, noisy inputs, model limitations, or hardware-level errors.
  • Sensor quality and analog front-end performance influence the quality of data available to the model.
  • ADC resolution, sampling rate, quantization, and linearity can affect the final AI/ML decision.
  • False positives and false negatives have different consequences depending on the application.
  • Reliable AI requires coordinated design across sensors, analog circuits, data conversion, models, digital processing, and system validation.
  • Mixed-signal design is an important contributor to reliable AI/ML systems, especially in edge and sensor-based applications.
